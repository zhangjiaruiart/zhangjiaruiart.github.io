Coral Planting Project

Documentary / 2025 / Xisha Islands

In 2025, I had the privilege of participating in a marine scientific expedition and ecological restoration initiative organized by a Chinese oceanographic research team. Traveling to the Xisha Islands, I worked closely with marine scientists and professional divers to complete the transplantation of a total of 24,000 coral seedlings. The project addressed the degradation of coral reefs and structural damage to local marine ecosystems through a combination of scientific assessment, meticulous planting, and long-term monitoring, aiming to facilitate the gradual recovery of reef systems. Through direct involvement in this process, I gained a profound understanding of the critical role coral reefs play within marine ecosystems. They are not only foundational to marine biodiversity but also serve as essential ecological barriers that mitigate the impacts of climate change and human disturbance. Coral restoration is not a one-time technical intervention, but a long-term ecological practice that depends on time, environmental conditions, and sustained responsibility. What is at stake is not the survival of a single species, but the regenerative capacity and resilience of an entire ecological network. This hands-on experience clarified for me that environmental protection is not an abstract ethical stance, but a form of practical engagement that requires bodily participation, scientific collaboration, and long-term commitment. At the same time, this experience fundamentally reshaped my artistic methodology and position. The temporal nature, uncertainty, and agency of non-human actors evident in the scientific restoration process compelled me to reconsider the role and responsibility of art within an ecological context. Art, in this sense, is no longer merely a representation or symbol of nature, but can function as a perceptual and reflective apparatus that operates in parallel with scientific practice. By attending to processes, relationships, and ecological dynamics, artistic practice can invite audiences to re-situate themselves within the environmental systems they inhabit. In this way, coral restoration has become a vital experiential foundation for my ecological art practice, enabling artistic work to emerge from concrete ecological action and to maintain a sustained and meaningful connection with environmental protection.
